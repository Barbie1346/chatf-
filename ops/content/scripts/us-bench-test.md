# Bench Test — episode 1

Title: Test one AI writing tool on one brief, and keep the client's name out of it
Chosen from:
1. Test one AI writing tool on one brief, and keep the client's name out of it
2. A 30-minute bench for any new AI writing tool
3. Stop pasting real customer emails into a demo
4. The scorecard has four lines and no leaderboard
5. What a screen recording must show before you trust a draft

Cold open: a laptop with a sticky note over the email list. The note says "fake brief." The first line is "If the tool only looks good on a customer's private email, you have not tested it. You have leaked it."

This episode is original. Original footage is the screen recording of a document created for this video. No third-party footage. The tool under test is named on screen only as "the editor," because the method is the point and a sponsor read is not.

This is not legal advice and not personal advice. Bench Test does not tell you which product to buy. A workplace policy overrides this video. If your employer forbids a tool, the bench stops.

## The brief that is safe to type

The document on screen is fictional. Company: Northwind Paper, a name made up for the episode. Task: turn five bullet points about a delayed shipment into a 120-word customer note. The bullets contain no real address, no real order number, and no real person. The video says, out loud, that a real order number is the thing you leave in the private system.

Why this brief: it has a tone constraint (apologize without admitting a legal fault), a length constraint, and a fact constraint (the new date is 12 May, a prop date). A tool that invents a refund has failed, even if the prose is smooth. That failure is the lesson.

## The four lines on the scorecard

Line one, fidelity. Did every prop fact survive? The date, the product (a ream of paper), and the absence of a refund. The reviewer highlights misses in red on the document. The video does not call the model a liar. It marks the sentence.

Line two, constraint. Is the note under 120 words? The word count is a software counter on screen, read aloud.

Line three, residue. Did the draft add a promise the brief did not authorize? A coupon, a phone number, a legal admission. Residue is a fail even when fidelity passed.

Line four, time. Minutes from paste to a draft a human would actually send, including the human edit. The clock is on screen. A fast bad draft scores worse than a slower clean one, because the edit is part of the work.

There is no fifth line for "vibes," and there is no ranking against a second product in this episode. The next episode runs the same brief through a second editor. Comparing two tools on two different briefs is how a bench lies.

## What the recording must include

The address bar, so a viewer can see the product page rather than a cropped mystery window. The moment before paste, so the brief is visible. The moment after the draft, uncut long enough to read. The human edit, with the deleted sentences left on screen as strikethrough. If the tool's terms forbid recording, the episode does not record it, and the description says the bench was skipped.

Background music, if any is added later, has to be music the desk has a license for. This script does not attach a track. Silence is an acceptable master.

## How the episode stays inside the rules

The scorecard changes when the brief changes. A later video can keep this brief and swap the editor, or keep the editor and change the brief, but not both, or the comparison is noise. The description lists the fictional company name so viewers do not search for a real Northwind Paper shipment. No customer data is reconstructed to make the demo feel authentic.

## Close

Read the sticky note again. One brief. Fake data. Four lines. A human still sends the email. The following episode changes only the editor, not the brief, so the scorecard can be compared.
